Part 1Learning objectives checklist

These are the official learning objectives for Chapters 1–3, word for word. Tick each item when you can do it without notes. The ticks are saved in this browser.

Chapter 1 · Complex numbers (Lesson 1)

  • Perform arithmetic with complex numbers, including addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, powers, and roots.
  • Represent and interpret complex numbers geometrically in the complex plane.
  • Determine the complex conjugate, magnitude (modulus), and argument (angle) of a complex number.
  • Use Euler’s formula to convert between complex exponentials and trigonometric functions.
  • Convert between rectangular and polar forms and use polar form to perform operations with complex numbers.
  • Determine the roots of unity and represent them on the unit circle.

Chapter 1 · Linear spaces and inner products (Lesson 2)

  • State the defining properties of a linear space and determine whether a given set with specified operations forms a linear space.
  • Determine whether a set of vectors or functions is linearly independent.
  • Compute inner products for finite-dimensional vectors and for functions.
  • Use an inner product to compute norms and distances.
  • Determine whether a set of vectors or functions is orthogonal or orthonormal.

Chapter 1 · Orthogonal expansions and approximation (Lesson 2)

  • Determine the coefficients of a function’s expansion in an orthogonal or orthonormal basis.
  • Apply Parseval’s equality to relate the norm of a function to the coefficients of its expansion.
  • Approximate a function using a finite subset of basis functions and quantify the resulting approximation error.

Chapter 2 · Fourier-series representation (Lesson 3)

  • Define the space \(L^2\) and determine whether a given function belongs to \(L^2\) on a specified interval.
  • Determine the period, frequency, and fundamental frequency of a periodic function or Fourier series.
  • Calculate the Fourier-series coefficients \(a_k\) using integrals and construct the corresponding Fourier-series expansion.
  • Express a Fourier series in cosine-and-sine form and determine the relationship between the coefficients \((\alpha_k, \beta_k)\) and \(a_k\).
  • Express a Fourier series in harmonic form in terms of \(\gamma_k\) and relate this representation to other forms of the Fourier series.

Chapter 2 · Properties and efficient computation (Lesson 3)

  • Use properties of real-valued functions to determine relationships among their Fourier-series coefficients.
  • Use even or odd symmetry to simplify the calculation of Fourier-series coefficients.
  • Derive the Fourier series of a new function from the Fourier series of a known function.
  • Determine the Fourier series of a derivative from a known Fourier series, when the appropriate conditions are satisfied.

Chapter 2 · Spectral interpretation and Parseval’s equality (Lesson 3)

  • Determine and interpret the spectrum of a periodic function from its Fourier-series coefficients.
  • Determine and interpret the power spectrum of a periodic function.
  • Apply Parseval’s equality to relate the mean-square value or norm of a periodic function to its Fourier-series coefficients.
  • Explain the relationship between a periodic function and a function defined over a limited interval, including periodic extension.

Chapter 2 · Approximation, truncation, and convergence (Lesson 3)

  • Approximate a function using a finite number of terms from its Fourier series.
  • Calculate or characterize the error introduced by truncating a Fourier series.
  • Describe the Gibbs phenomenon qualitatively, including where it occurs and what happens as additional Fourier-series terms are included.

Chapter 3 · Discrete Fourier transform (Lesson 4)

  • Construct and evaluate a trigonometric polynomial.
  • Explain uniform sampling of a periodic function and relate sampled values to Fourier-series coefficients.
  • Apply the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) to convert a sequence of sampled values into frequency-domain coefficients.
  • Apply the inverse DFT to reconstruct sampled values from their frequency-domain coefficients.
  • Explain the purpose of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and its relationship to the DFT.
  • Compare the computational complexity of direct DFT calculation with FFT calculation.
  • Use sampled data and the DFT or FFT to calculate a discrete frequency spectrum.
  • Interpret FFT output by identifying the frequencies and relative strengths of signal components.

Part 2Formula sheet

Complex numbers

$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta,\quad z^n = r^ne^{in\theta}$$ $$z_k = \rho^{1/n}e^{i(\phi+2\pi k)/n}$$ $$\ln z = \ln|z| + i(\angle z + 2\pi k)$$ $$\alpha\cos\omega t + \beta\sin\omega t = \gamma\cos(\omega t - \psi)$$ $$\gamma e^{i\psi} = \alpha + i\beta$$

Inner products

$$(f,g) = \textstyle\sum f_k\bar g_k,\quad (f,g) = \frac1\tau\int_0^\tau f\bar g\,dt$$ $$a_k = \frac{(f,\varphi_k)}{\|\varphi_k\|^2},\quad \|f\|^2 = \sum|a_k|^2\|\varphi_k\|^2$$ $$\Big\|f - \sum_{k\le N}a_k\varphi_k\Big\|^2 = \sum_{k>N}|a_k|^2\|\varphi_k\|^2$$

Fourier series

$$f = \sum a_ke^{-ik\omega_0t},\quad a_k = \frac1\tau\int_0^\tau fe^{ik\omega_0t}dt$$ $$\alpha_k = a_k + a_{-k},\quad \beta_k = i(a_{-k} - a_k)$$ $$a_k = \tfrac{\alpha_k + i\beta_k}{2},\quad \gamma_k = \sqrt{\alpha_k^2+\beta_k^2},\quad \psi_k = \angle(\alpha_k+i\beta_k)$$ $$\|f\|^2 = \sum|a_k|^2 = a_0^2 + \tfrac12\sum(\alpha_k^2+\beta_k^2)$$

Properties (notes' convention)

$$f(t - T)\to a_ke^{ik\omega_0T},\quad e^{-in\omega_0t}f\to a_{k-n}$$ $$f'\to -ik\omega_0a_k,\quad \textstyle\int(f - a_0)\to \frac{ia_k}{k\omega_0}$$ $$\bar f\to \overline{a_{-k}},\quad fh \to \textstyle\sum_m a_mc_{k-m}$$

DFT

$$F_\nu[j,k] = e^{-2\pi ijk/\nu},\quad F_\nu^{-1} = \tfrac1\nu\overline{F_\nu}$$ $$\texttt{ifft}(y) = [a_0,\dots,a_n,a_{-m},\dots,a_{-1}]$$ $$\nu\ge 2K+1,\quad \texttt{ifft} \to \textstyle\sum_\ell a_{k+\ell\nu}$$

Spectra in Hz

$$\Delta f = F_s/N,\quad \text{index } k \to r = k-1$$ $$f = rF_s/N\ (r\le N/2),\quad (r-N)F_s/N\ (r > N/2)$$ $$\text{FFT: } \nu\log_2\nu \text{ vs } \nu^2$$

Part 3Common mistakes

  • Wrong quadrant: \(\angle(-1 - i) = -3\pi/4\), not \(\pi/4\). Sketch the point before taking an arctangent.
  • Missing conjugate: \((f,g) = \sum f_k\overline{g_k}\), and \(\|1+i\|^2 = 2\), not \((1+i)^2 = 2i\).
  • Missing \(1/\tau\): in \(L^2(0,\tau)\) the course normalizes inner products and coefficients by \(1/\tau\).
  • Sign convention: the notes use \(e^{-ik\omega_0t}\) in the series. Under it, \(\sin t\) has \(a_1 = i/2\), not \(-i/2\).
  • Doubling \(a_0\): \(\alpha_k = 2\Re a_k\) only for \(k \ge 1\). The constant term is \(a_0\).
  • Dividing by zero: formulas like \(\frac{1}{k-1}\) need the \(k = 1\) case (and \(k = 0\)) done separately.
  • Wrong \(\omega_0\): \(\omega_0\) comes from the period of the whole signal. In a signal of period 1, \(\cos4\pi t\) is harmonic 2.
  • MATLAB indices: a(1) is \(a_0\), a(end) is \(a_{-1}\), and fft index \(k\) is bin \(k-1\).

Part 4Exam-style problems

Four multi-part problems in the format of the exam, one per chapter section. Work each on paper, then open the solution.

Problems

(a) Find \(|\gamma|\), \(\angle\gamma\in(-\pi,\pi]\), and \(\Re\gamma\), \(\Im\gamma\) for \(\gamma = \dfrac{(1 + i\sqrt3)^5}{2i}\).

(b) Find all solutions of \(z^3 + 27 = 0\).

(c) Find the principal value of \((1+i)^i\).

(d) Write \(-\cos 3t - \sqrt3\sin 3t\) in the form \(\gamma\cos(3t - \psi)\).

Solution

(a) \(1 + i\sqrt3 = 2e^{i\pi/3}\), so the numerator is \(32e^{i5\pi/3}\). Dividing by \(2i = 2e^{i\pi/2}\) gives \(\gamma = 16e^{i(5\pi/3 - \pi/2)} = 16e^{i7\pi/6} = 16e^{-i5\pi/6}\). So \(|\gamma| = 16\), \(\angle\gamma = -5\pi/6\), \(\gamma = -8\sqrt3 - 8i\).

(b) \(z^3 = -27 = 27e^{i\pi}\), so \(z_k = 3e^{i(\pi + 2\pi k)/3}\): \(z = \tfrac32 + \tfrac{3\sqrt3}2i,\ -3,\ \tfrac32 - \tfrac{3\sqrt3}2i\).

(c) \(\ln(1+i) = \ln\sqrt2 + i\pi/4\), so \((1+i)^i = e^{i\ln\sqrt2 - \pi/4} = e^{-\pi/4}\big(\cos(\ln\sqrt2) + i\sin(\ln\sqrt2)\big) \approx 0.429 + 0.155i\).

(d) The phasor is \(\alpha + i\beta = -1 - i\sqrt3 = 2e^{-i2\pi/3}\) (third quadrant). So the signal is \(2\cos(3t + 2\pi/3)\), i.e. \(\gamma = 2\), \(\psi = -2\pi/3\).

Work in \(L^2(0,1)\) with \((f,g) = \int_0^1 f\bar g\,dt\).

(a) Show that \(1\) and \(t - \tfrac12\) are orthogonal, and find their norms.

(b) Find \(b\) and \(c\) so that \(t^2 + bt + c\) is orthogonal to both \(1\) and \(t - \tfrac12\).

(c) Find the best approximation of \(f(t) = t^2\) by a line \(c_0 + c_1(t - \tfrac12)\), and the squared error.

Solution

(a) \(\int_0^1 (t - \tfrac12)dt = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0\). \(\|1\|^2 = 1\) and \(\|t - \tfrac12\|^2 = \int_0^1(t-\tfrac12)^2dt = \tfrac1{12}\).

(b) Orthogonal to 1: \(\tfrac13 + \tfrac b2 + c = 0\). Orthogonal to \(t - \tfrac12\): \(\int_0^1 t^2(t - \tfrac12)dt + b\int_0^1 t(t-\tfrac12)dt = \tfrac1{12} + \tfrac b{12} = 0\), and the \(c\) term vanishes by (a). So \(b = -1\) and \(c = \tfrac16\): the polynomial is \(t^2 - t + \tfrac16\).

(c) The basis is orthogonal, so \(c_0 = (t^2, 1)/\|1\|^2 = \tfrac13\) and \(c_1 = (t^2, t-\tfrac12)/\|t - \tfrac12\|^2 = \tfrac{1/12}{1/12} = 1\). The best line is \(\tfrac13 + (t - \tfrac12) = t - \tfrac16\). The error is \(\|t^2\|^2 - c_0^2\cdot1 - c_1^2\cdot\tfrac1{12} = \tfrac15 - \tfrac19 - \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac1{180}\). The leftover \(t^2 - t + \tfrac16\) is exactly the polynomial from (b), orthogonal to the line, as it must be.

Let \(f\) be the 4-periodic pulse \(f(t) = 1\) on \([0,1)\), \(f(t) = 0\) on \([1,4)\), with \(f = \sum a_ke^{-ik\omega_0t}\).

(a) Find \(\omega_0\), \(a_0\) and \(a_k\) for \(k\ne0\).

(b) Find \(\alpha_1\) and \(\beta_1\).

(c) Find \(\|f\|^2\) and the mean-square error \(\|f - p_2\|^2\) of the partial sum with \(|k|\le2\).

(d) What does the series equal at \(t = 1\)?

(e) Find the coefficients of \(g(t) = f(t - 1)\) and of \(h(t) = e^{-i\pi t}f(t)\).

Solution

(a) \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/4 = \pi/2\). \(a_0 = \tfrac14\) (the average). For \(k\ne0\):

$$a_k = \frac14\int_0^1 e^{ik\pi t/2}dt = \frac14\cdot\frac{e^{ik\pi/2} - 1}{ik\pi/2} = \frac{e^{ik\pi/2} - 1}{2\pi ik}.$$

(b) \(a_1 = \frac{i - 1}{2\pi i} = \frac{1 + i}{2\pi}\), so \(\alpha_1 = 2\Re a_1 = \frac1\pi\) and \(\beta_1 = 2\Im a_1 = \frac1\pi\). (Check: \(\alpha_1 = \frac24\int_0^1\cos\frac{\pi t}2dt = \frac1\pi\).)

(c) \(\|f\|^2 = \frac14\int_0^1 1\,dt = \frac14\). Since \(|a_k|^2 = \frac{2 - 2\cos(k\pi/2)}{4\pi^2k^2}\), we get \(|a_{\pm1}|^2 = \frac1{2\pi^2}\) and \(|a_{\pm2}|^2 = \frac1{4\pi^2}\). Then

$$\|f - p_2\|^2 = \frac14 - \frac1{16} - 2\left(\frac1{2\pi^2} + \frac1{4\pi^2}\right) = \frac3{16} - \frac3{2\pi^2} \approx 0.0355.$$

(d) \(f\) jumps from 1 to 0 at \(t = 1\), so the series gives the midpoint \(\tfrac12\).

(e) Delay by \(T = 1\): \(b_k = a_ke^{ik\omega_0} = a_ke^{ik\pi/2} = i^ka_k\). Modulation: \(e^{-i\pi t} = e^{-i2\omega_0t}\), so \(c_k = a_{k-2}\).

(a) \(g(t) = 1 + 4\cos3t - 2\sin t\) is sampled at \(\nu = 8\) points \(t_j = 2\pi j/8\). Give a = ifft(g).

(b) Repeat with \(\nu = 5\). Which signal does the result describe?

(c) A real signal is sampled \(N = 4096\) times at \(F_s = 2000\) Hz, and abs(fft(x)) peaks only at indices 615 and 3483. Describe the signal.

(d) If instead \(x\) samples a single complex exponential \(\eta e^{-i2\pi f t}\) and fft peaks only at index 3483, what is \(f\)?

Solution

(a) \(a_0 = 1\), \(a_{\pm3} = 2\), and \(-2\sin t\) gives \(a_1 = -2\cdot\tfrac i2 = -i\), \(a_{-1} = i\). With \(\nu = 8\) the order is \([a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4, a_{-3}, a_{-2}, a_{-1}]\):

$$\texttt{a} = [\,1,\ -i,\ 0,\ 2,\ 0,\ 2,\ 0,\ i\,].$$

(b) \(\nu = 5 < 7\), so harmonic \(3\) aliases to \(3 - 5 = -2\) and \(-3\) to \(2\). With order \([a_0, a_1, a_2, a_{-2}, a_{-1}]\), a \(= [1, -i, 2, 2, i]\), which describes \(1 + 4\cos 2t - 2\sin t\).

(c) Bins \(r = 614\) and \(3482 = 4096 - 614\): a ± pair, so one real cosine (with some phase) at \(614\cdot2000/4096 \approx 299.8\) Hz.

(d) Bin 3482 is the negative frequency \((3482 - 4096)\cdot\frac{2000}{4096} \approx -299.8\) Hz. MATLAB's fft kernel is \(e^{-i2\pi rj/N}\), so a signal \(e^{-i2\pi ft}\) peaks at \(-f\). Hence \(f \approx +299.8\) Hz.
