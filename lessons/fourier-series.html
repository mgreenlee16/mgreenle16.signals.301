Lesson 3 · Exam 1

Fourier Series

A periodic signal is a sum of harmonics: a constant, a sinusoid at the fundamental frequency, and sinusoids at its integer multiples. This lesson computes the coefficients, moves between the exponential, cosine–sine and harmonic forms, reads off spectra and power with Parseval, measures the error of a truncated series, explains the Gibbs phenomenon, and collects the shift, modulation, derivative and integral rules. It is the center of Exam 1.

Section 3.1Periodic signals

A signal is periodic with period \(\tau\) if \(f(t+\tau) = f(t)\) for all \(t\). The smallest such \(\tau\) is the fundamental period and \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/\tau\) is the fundamental (angular) frequency. A sum of sinusoids at frequencies \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots\) is periodic only if all the frequencies are integer multiples of one \(\omega_0\); then \(\omega_0\) is their greatest common divisor. For example, \(\cos 6\pi t + \sin 4\pi t\) has \(\omega_0 = \gcd(6\pi, 4\pi) = 2\pi\), so \(\tau = 1\). But \(\cos t + \cos\sqrt2 t\) is not periodic at all.

Section 3.2The Fourier series and its coefficients

Lesson 2 showed that \(\{e^{-ik\omega_0t}\}_{k\in\mathbb Z}\) is an orthonormal basis for \(L^2(0,\tau)\) with \((f,g) = \frac1\tau\int_0^\tau f\bar g\,dt\). Expanding in it gives the Fourier series, with coefficients equal to inner products:

$$f(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a_k e^{-ik\omega_0t},\qquad a_k = (f, e^{-ik\omega_0t}) = \frac1\tau\int_0^\tau f(t)\,e^{ik\omega_0t}\,dt.$$

Any interval of length \(\tau\) can be used for the integral. \(a_0\) is the average value of \(f\). Because the course follows Frazho's notes, the series uses \(e^{-ik\omega_0t}\) and the coefficient integral uses \(e^{+ik\omega_0t}\).

Two sign conventionsThe lecture slides on properties use the engineering convention \(f = \sum a_ke^{+jk\omega_0t}\), \(a_k = \frac1\tau\int f e^{-jk\omega_0t}dt\). The two conventions swap \(a_k \leftrightarrow a_{-k}\). Rules that involve \(k\) change sign: the slides' derivative rule \(b_k = jk\omega_0a_k\) becomes \(b_k = -ik\omega_0a_k\) here. On an exam, use whichever convention the problem states; this site uses the notes' convention throughout.

For a real signal, conjugating the coefficient integral gives \(a_{-k} = \overline{a_k}\), so \(|a_{-k}| = |a_k|\). If \(f\) is also even, every \(a_k\) is real; if it is odd, every \(a_k\) is purely imaginary. Checking symmetry first often halves the work.

Section 3.3Cosine–sine and harmonic forms

Grouping the \(k\) and \(-k\) terms gives the cosine–sine form, and combining each cosine–sine pair with the phasor rule of Lesson 1 gives the harmonic form:

$$f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^\infty\big(\alpha_k\cos k\omega_0t + \beta_k\sin k\omega_0t\big) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \gamma_k\cos(k\omega_0t - \psi_k),$$ $$\alpha_k = a_k + a_{-k},\qquad \beta_k = i(a_{-k} - a_k),\qquad a_k = \frac{\alpha_k + i\beta_k}{2},\qquad \gamma_k = \sqrt{\alpha_k^2+\beta_k^2},\quad \psi_k = \angle(\alpha_k + i\beta_k).$$

For real \(f\), \(\alpha_k = 2\Re a_k\), \(\beta_k = 2\Im a_k\) and \(\gamma_k = 2|a_k|\). The direct formulas are \(\alpha_k = \frac2\tau\int_0^\tau f\cos k\omega_0t\,dt\) and \(\beta_k = \frac2\tau\int_0^\tau f\sin k\omega_0t\,dt\). In MATLAB terms, \(\gamma_k\) is the amplitude and \(\psi_k\) the phase lag of the \(k\)-th harmonic.

Section 3.4Reading coefficients off a trigonometric polynomial

When \(f\) is already a finite sum of sinusoids, no integral is needed. Rewrite each term with \(\cos\theta = \tfrac12(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})\) and \(\sin\theta = \tfrac1{2i}(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta})\), and match \(e^{-ik\omega_0t}\):

$$\cos k\omega_0 t \;\Rightarrow\; a_{\pm k} = \tfrac12,\qquad \sin k\omega_0t \;\Rightarrow\; a_{k} = \tfrac{i}{2},\; a_{-k} = -\tfrac{i}{2}.$$

Products and powers are first turned into sums with identities: \(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]\), \(\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos2\theta\), \(\sin^3\theta = \tfrac34\sin\theta - \tfrac14\sin3\theta\). Remember that \(\omega_0\) is set by the period of the whole signal, so \(\cos 4\pi t\) in a signal of period 1 is harmonic \(k = 2\).

Section 3.5Spectrum and Parseval

The amplitude spectrum is the plot of \(|a_k|\) against \(k\) (or against frequency \(k\omega_0\)); the power spectrum plots \(|a_k|^2\). For real signals both are symmetric about \(k=0\). Parseval's formula says the average power in the time domain equals the total power in the harmonics:

$$\|f\|^2 = \frac1\tau\int_0^\tau|f(t)|^2\,dt = \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty |a_k|^2 = |a_0|^2 + \frac12\sum_{k=1}^\infty\big(\alpha_k^2 + \beta_k^2\big)\ \ (\text{real } f).$$

Parseval works in both directions. It gives \(\|f\|^2\) of a trigonometric polynomial without integrating. Going the other way, it evaluates infinite sums: the triangle wave's \(\|f\|^2 = \tfrac13\) yields \(\sum_{k\text{ odd}} 1/k^4 = \pi^4/96\).

Section 3.6Truncation, convergence and Gibbs

The partial sum \(p_n(t) = \sum_{k=-n}^n a_ke^{-ik\omega_0t}\) is the best approximation of \(f\) by a trigonometric polynomial of degree \(n\) (Lesson 2), and its mean-square error is

$$\|f - p_n\|^2 = \|f\|^2 - \sum_{|k|\le n}|a_k|^2 = \sum_{|k|>n}|a_k|^2.$$

How fast this goes to zero depends on smoothness. A signal with a jump has \(|a_k| \sim 1/k\) (square wave, sawtooth). A continuous periodic signal with a kink has \(|a_k| \sim 1/k^2\) (triangle), and smoother signals decay faster still.

Pointwise, Dirichlet's theorem says that for piecewise smooth \(f\) the series converges to \(f(t)\) where \(f\) is continuous and to the midpoint \(\tfrac12[f(t^-) + f(t^+)]\) at a jump. The jump points include the ends of the period when \(f(0) \ne f(\tau)\): the sawtooth \(t\) on \([0,2\pi)\) has a series that equals \(\pi\) at \(t = 0\). Near a jump the partial sums always overshoot by about 8.9% of the jump, however many terms are kept. This is the Gibbs phenomenon. The overshoot narrows as \(n\) grows, so the energy of the error still goes to zero, but its peak height does not.

Section 3.7Properties: shift, modulation, derivative, integral

Let \(f = \sum a_ke^{-ik\omega_0t}\) and write \(b_k\) for the coefficients of a new signal \(g\). Each rule follows by substituting the series and regrouping:

Section 3.8Signals that do not fit the window

Any \(f\) in \(L^2(0,\tau)\) has a Fourier series on that interval, even if it is not periodic. The series then represents the periodic extension of the piece in the window. For \(\cos bt\) on \((0, 2\pi)\) with \(b\) an integer, the window holds whole cycles and the spectrum has two spikes at \(k = \pm b\). If \(b\) is not an integer, the periodic extension jumps at the window edge. The power then spreads into every harmonic, with the largest coefficients near \(\pm b\). This leakage appears again in the DFT whenever a sampled record does not contain whole cycles.

Section 3.9Worked examples

Worked examples

Find the Fourier series of the square wave \(f = 1\) on \([0,\pi)\), \(f = -1\) on \([\pi, 2\pi)\), in exponential and cosine–sine form.

Solution

\(\tau = 2\pi\), \(\omega_0 = 1\), and \(a_0 = 0\) (the average). For \(k\ne0\),

$$a_k = \frac1{2\pi}\left[\int_0^\pi e^{ikt}dt - \int_\pi^{2\pi}e^{ikt}dt\right] = \frac{1}{2\pi ik}\big[(e^{ik\pi}-1) - (1 - e^{ik\pi})\big] = \frac{(-1)^k - 1}{\pi ik}.$$

So \(a_k = 0\) for even \(k\) and \(a_k = \frac{-2}{\pi ik} = \frac{2i}{\pi k}\) for odd \(k\). The signal is odd about \(t = 0\) (in its periodic extension), so the coefficients are purely imaginary. Then \(\alpha_k = 2\Re a_k = 0\) and \(\beta_k = 2\Im a_k = \frac{4}{\pi k}\):

$$f(t) = \frac4\pi\left(\sin t + \frac{\sin3t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5t}{5} + \cdots\right).$$

\(f(t) = 3 + 4\cos2t - 2\sin3t\). Find every nonzero \(a_k\), and \(\|f\|^2\) on \((0,2\pi)\).

Solution

\(a_0 = 3\). \(4\cos2t = 2e^{-i2t} + 2e^{i2t}\), so \(a_{2} = a_{-2} = 2\). \(-2\sin 3t = \frac{-2}{2i}(e^{i3t} - e^{-i3t}) = i e^{i3t} - ie^{-i3t}\), so \(a_3 = -i\) and \(a_{-3} = i\).

Check against the cosine–sine form: \(\beta_3 = i(a_{-3} - a_3) = i(2i) = -2\). ✓ By Parseval, \(\|f\|^2 = 9 + 4 + 4 + 1 + 1 = 19\) \(= 9 + \tfrac12(16 + 4)\). ✓

The triangle wave \(f = 2t\) on \([0,\tfrac12]\), \(2(1-t)\) on \([\tfrac12, 1]\) has \(a_0 = \tfrac12\) and \(a_k = \frac{(-1)^k-1}{\pi^2k^2}\). Verify Parseval and find the error of \(p_1\).

Solution

Directly, \(\|f\|^2 = 2\int_0^{1/2}4t^2dt = \tfrac13\). From the coefficients, only odd \(k\) survive, with \(|a_k|^2 = \frac{4}{\pi^4k^4}\):

$$\sum|a_k|^2 = \frac14 + 2\cdot\frac4{\pi^4}\sum_{k\text{ odd}}\frac1{k^4} = \frac14 + \frac8{\pi^4}\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{96} = \frac14 + \frac1{12} = \frac13.\ ✓$$

Keeping \(|k|\le1\): \(\|f - p_1\|^2 = \tfrac13 - \tfrac14 - 2\cdot\frac{4}{\pi^4} = \tfrac1{12} - \frac8{\pi^4} \approx 0.00121\). Three terms already capture 99.6% of the power, because the coefficients decay like \(1/k^2\).

\(f\) has period \(\tau = 2\) and coefficients \(a_k\). Find the coefficients of \(g(t) = f(t - \tfrac12)\), of \(h = f'\), and of \(e^{-i2\pi t}f(t)\).

Solution

\(\omega_0 = \pi\). Delay: \(b_k = a_ke^{ik\omega_0T} = a_ke^{ik\pi/2} = i^k a_k\). Derivative: \(b_k = -ik\pi\,a_k\). Modulation: \(e^{-i2\pi t} = e^{-i2\omega_0t}\) is \(n = 2\), so \(b_k = a_{k-2}\).

With the slides' \(e^{+jk\omega_0t}\) convention, the same problems give \(e^{-jk\omega_0T}a_k\) and \(jk\omega_0a_k\). The signs flip with the convention.

Find the coefficients of \(f(t)^2\) where \(f = \cos t + \sin t\), both by expanding and by convolving coefficients.

Solution

Expand: \(f^2 = \cos^2t + 2\sin t\cos t + \sin^2t = 1 + \sin2t\), so \(c_0 = 1\), \(c_2 = \tfrac i2\), \(c_{-2} = -\tfrac i2\).

Convolve: \(a_{1} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac i2\) and \(a_{-1} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac i2\). Then \(c_0 = a_1a_{-1} + a_{-1}a_1 = 2\cdot\tfrac12 = 1\), \(c_2 = a_1a_1 = \tfrac14(1+i)^2 = \tfrac i2\), \(c_{-2} = a_{-1}^2 = -\tfrac i2\). ✓

The sawtooth \(f(t) = t\) on \([0, 2\pi)\). To what does its Fourier series converge at \(t = 0\), \(t = \pi\) and \(t = 2\pi\)? What is the largest value of \(p_n(t)\) near \(t = 2\pi\) for large \(n\)?

Solution

The periodic extension is continuous at \(\pi\), so the series gives \(\pi\) there. At \(t = 0\) and \(2\pi\) it jumps from \(2\pi\) down to \(0\); the series gives the midpoint \(\pi\) at both. Just before the jump the partial sums overshoot by about 8.9% of the jump size \(2\pi\), so \(\max p_n \approx 2\pi + 0.089\cdot2\pi \approx 6.85\).
