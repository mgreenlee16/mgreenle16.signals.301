Lesson 4 · Exam 1

The DFT & FFT

A computer never sees \(f(t)\), only samples of it. The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) turns \(\nu\) samples into Fourier coefficients and back. For a trigonometric polynomial with enough samples the result is exact; for anything else it is an approximation, and aliasing explains the error. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes the same thing in \(\nu\log\nu\) operations instead of \(\nu^2\). This lesson also covers MATLAB's ordering of fft/ifft output and how to read physical frequencies off a spectrum, the skills behind the last exam problem.

Section 4.1Sampling a trigonometric polynomial

Take a trigonometric polynomial of period \(\tau\) and sample it at \(\nu\) equally spaced points in one period:

$$p(t) = \sum_{k} a_ke^{-ik\omega_0t},\qquad t_j = \frac{j\tau}{\nu},\quad j = 0, 1, \dots, \nu-1.$$

Since \(k\omega_0t_j = 2\pi jk/\nu\), each sample is \(p(t_j) = \sum_k a_k\lambda^{jk}\) with \(\lambda = e^{-2\pi i/\nu}\), a root of unity (Lesson 1). Because \(\lambda^\nu = 1\), harmonic \(k\) and harmonic \(k \pm \nu\) produce identical samples. So only \(\nu\) distinct harmonics can be told apart, and the natural choice is the \(\nu\) closest to zero.

Section 4.2The DFT matrix and its inverse

Collect the samples in \(y = [p(t_0),\dots,p(t_{\nu-1})]^T\). The relation between samples and coefficients is a matrix product, \(y = F_\nu u\), with the DFT matrix

$$F_\nu[j,k] = e^{-2\pi ijk/\nu}\quad(j,k = 0,\dots,\nu-1),\qquad F_4 = \begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1\\1&-i&-1&i\\1&-1&1&-1\\1&i&-1&-i\end{bmatrix}.$$

Its columns are orthogonal with squared norm \(\nu\): the inner product of columns \(k\) and \(m\) is a geometric sum of the roots of unity \(e^{-2\pi ij(k-m)/\nu}\), which is \(\nu\) if \(k = m\) and \(0\) otherwise. So \(F_\nu^*F_\nu = \nu I\) and

$$F_\nu^{-1} = \frac1\nu F_\nu^* = \frac1\nu\overline{F_\nu},\qquad u = F_\nu^{-1}y,\qquad u_k = \frac1\nu\sum_{j=0}^{\nu-1} y_j\,e^{+2\pi ijk/\nu}.$$

(\(F_\nu\) is symmetric, so \(F_\nu^* = \overline{F_\nu}\).) The last formula is the Riemann-sum version of \(a_k = \frac1\tau\int_0^\tau f e^{ik\omega_0t}dt\), which is why the coefficients come out of \(F^{-1}\).

Section 4.3MATLAB's fft and ifft

MATLAB computes exactly these two products: fft(y) \(= F_\nu y\) and ifft(y) \(= F_\nu^{-1}y\). So ifft of the samples gives the Fourier coefficients in the course's convention. The only subtlety is the order. Slot \(k\) (counting from 0) holds the harmonic \(k\) if \(k \le \nu/2\), and the harmonic \(k - \nu\) otherwise:

$$\texttt{ifft}(y) = [\,a_0,\ a_1,\ \dots,\ a_n,\ a_{-m},\ \dots,\ a_{-1}\,],\qquad n = \lfloor\nu/2\rfloor,\quad m = \lceil\nu/2\rceil - 1.$$

MATLAB counts from 1, so a(1) is \(a_0\), a(k+1) is \(a_k\), and \(a_{-k}\) sits in a(ν−k+1), for example a(end) \(= a_{-1}\). (fftshift reorders the output to run from negative to positive harmonics.) For a real signal, the negative slots are the conjugates of the positive ones. Converting back to cosine–sine form uses \(\alpha_k = 2\Re a_k\) and \(\beta_k = 2\Im a_k\), with \(a_0\) not doubled.

Section 4.4Nyquist and aliasing

A trigonometric polynomial with harmonics \(-K,\dots,K\) has \(2K+1\) unknown coefficients, so it is recovered exactly from \(\nu\) samples when

$$\nu \ge 2K + 1\qquad\text{(Nyquist condition).}$$

With fewer samples, harmonic \(k\) is indistinguishable from \(k \bmod \nu\) folded into \((-\nu/2,\nu/2]\): it is aliased. The notes' example: \(2\sin4t\) sampled at \(\nu = 5\) points of \([0,2\pi)\) gives exactly the same numbers as \(-2\sin t\), because \(4 \equiv -1 \pmod 5\) and \(\sin(-t) = -\sin t\). ifft then reports a harmonic that is not in the signal at all.

Section 4.5Approximating Fourier coefficients of any signal

For a general periodic \(f\) (not a trigonometric polynomial), sample it and apply ifft. Slot \(k\) then holds not \(a_k\) but the sum of every harmonic that aliases onto it:

$$\frac1\nu\sum_{j=0}^{\nu-1}f(t_j)e^{2\pi ijk/\nu} = \sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty} a_{k+\ell\nu} = a_k + a_{k+\nu} + a_{k-\nu} + \cdots.$$

If the coefficients decay quickly, the extra terms are small for \(|k| \ll \nu/2\). So ifft approximates the low harmonics well and the ones near \(\pm\nu/2\) poorly. Doubling \(\nu\) is the cure. In MATLAB, sample on t = (0:nu-1)*tau/nu, which leaves out the endpoint \(t = \tau\) (it would repeat \(t = 0\)). At a jump, use the midpoint value.

Section 4.6The FFT

Computing \(F_\nu y\) directly takes about \(\nu^2\) complex multiplications. The fast Fourier transform (Cooley–Tukey, 1965) splits a length-\(\nu\) DFT into two of length \(\nu/2\), one for the even samples and one for the odd, plus \(\nu/2\) "twiddle" multiplications by powers of \(\lambda\). Repeating the split \(\log_2\nu\) times gives a cost of about \(\nu\log_2\nu\). The speed-up factor is \(\nu/\log_2\nu\): 102 for \(\nu = 1024\), 341 for \(\nu = 4096\), and over 50,000 for a million points. That speed is why spectral analysis is practical. MATLAB's fft is fastest when \(\nu\) is a power of 2, but it works for any length.

Section 4.7Spectral analysis in hertz

In practice data come as \(N\) samples at a sampling rate \(F_s\) (Hz), so the record lasts \(T = N/F_s\) seconds. That record is the "period" \(\tau\), and harmonic \(r\) has frequency \(r/T = rF_s/N\) Hz. The bin spacing \(\Delta f = F_s/N\) is the frequency resolution. To read a spectrum:

  1. MATLAB index \(k\) is bin \(r = k - 1\).
  2. Bins \(r \le N/2\) are frequency \(rF_s/N\); bins \(r > N/2\) are the negative frequency \((r - N)F_s/N\).
  3. A real cosine \(\xi\cos2\pi f t\) gives a pair of peaks at \(\pm f\), each of height \(\xi/2\) in ifft or \(N\xi/2\) in fft. Count pairs to count cosines.
  4. A complex exponential gives one peak. The sign matters: MATLAB's fft kernel is \(e^{-i2\pi rj/N}\), so for a signal written \(\eta e^{-i2\pi f t}\) the fft peak appears at frequency \(-f\). With ifft (the course's \(a_k\)) it appears at \(+f\).
  5. Frequencies above the Nyquist frequency \(F_s/2\) fold back: a tone at \(f\) shows up at \(|f - \ell F_s|\) for the integer \(\ell\) that brings it into \([0, F_s/2]\).

If a tone does not complete a whole number of cycles in the record (\(fT\) is not an integer), its power leaks into neighboring bins, exactly as in Interactive 4 of Lesson 3.

Section 4.8Worked examples

Worked examples

Compute ifft([1 2 3 4]) by hand and identify each entry as a Fourier coefficient.

Solution

\(\nu = 4\) and \(F_4^{-1} = \tfrac14\overline{F_4}\), so \(u_k = \tfrac14\sum_j y_j\,i^{jk}\):

$$u_0 = \tfrac{1+2+3+4}{4} = 2.5,\quad u_1 = \tfrac{1 + 2i - 3 - 4i}{4} = -\tfrac12 - \tfrac i2,\quad u_2 = \tfrac{1 - 2 + 3 - 4}{4} = -\tfrac12,\quad u_3 = \tfrac{1 - 2i - 3 + 4i}{4} = -\tfrac12 + \tfrac i2.$$

The order is \([a_0, a_1, a_2, a_{-1}]\). As expected for real data, \(a_{-1} = \overline{a_1}\), and \(a_0\) is the mean.

\(g(t) = 3 + 2\cos t - 4\sin 2t\) is sampled at \(t_j = 2\pi j/6\), \(j = 0,\dots,5\). What is a = ifft(g)?

Solution

The highest harmonic is 2 and \(\nu = 6 \ge 5\), so the result is exact. Read off the coefficients: \(a_0 = 3\), \(a_{\pm1} = 1\), and from \(-4\sin2t\): \(a_2 = -4\cdot\tfrac i2 = -2i\), \(a_{-2} = 2i\). Slot order for \(\nu = 6\) is \([a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3, a_{-2}, a_{-1}]\):

$$\texttt{a} = [\,3,\ 1,\ -2i,\ 0,\ 2i,\ 1\,].$$

Going backwards, a(2) \(= 1\) gives \(\alpha_1 = 2\), \(\beta_1 = 0\), and a(3) \(= -2i\) gives \(\alpha_2 = 0\), \(\beta_2 = -4\).

\(f(t) = 2\sin 4t\) is sampled at 5 points of \([0,2\pi)\). What does ifft return, and which signal does it describe?

Solution

The true coefficients are \(a_4 = i\), \(a_{-4} = -i\). With \(\nu = 5\), harmonic \(4\) aliases to \(4 - 5 = -1\) and \(-4\) to \(1\). So ifft gives \(a_{1} = -i\), \(a_{-1} = i\), i.e. [0, -i, 0, 0, i], which are the coefficients of \(-2\sin t\). The samples really are identical: \(\sin(4\cdot 2\pi j/5) = \sin(-2\pi j/5)\). Nyquist needs \(\nu \ge 9\).

A sum of real cosines is sampled \(N = 2048\) times at \(F_s = 1000\) Hz. abs(fft(x)).^2 has peaks at MATLAB indices 101, 301, 1749 and 1949. How many cosines, at what frequencies?

Solution

Bins \(r = 100, 300, 1748, 1948\). The last two are above \(N/2 = 1024\), so they are negative: \(1748 - 2048 = -300\) and \(1948 - 2048 = -100\). The peaks form two ± pairs, so there are 2 cosines, at

$$100\cdot\frac{1000}{2048} \approx 48.8\ \text{Hz},\qquad 300\cdot\frac{1000}{2048} \approx 146.5\ \text{Hz}.$$

How many times faster than the direct matrix product is an FFT of length \(2^{12}\)? Of length \(2^{20}\)?

Solution

The ratio is \(\nu^2/(\nu\log_2\nu) = \nu/\log_2\nu\): \(4096/12 \approx 341\), and \(1{,}048{,}576/20 \approx 52{,}429\).

SummaryKey equations

DFT matrix

$$F_\nu[j,k] = e^{-2\pi ijk/\nu},\quad F_\nu^{-1} = \tfrac1\nu\overline{F_\nu}$$ $$y = F_\nu u,\quad u = \texttt{ifft}(y)$$

MATLAB order

$$\texttt{ifft}(y) = [a_0, a_1,\dots,a_n, a_{-m},\dots,a_{-1}]$$ $$a_k \to \texttt{a(k+1)},\quad a_{-k}\to\texttt{a}(\nu-k+1)$$

Sampling

$$\nu \ge 2K+1,\qquad \texttt{ifft} \to \sum_\ell a_{k+\ell\nu}$$ $$\text{cost: } \nu\log_2\nu \text{ vs } \nu^2$$

Hertz

$$f_r = r\frac{F_s}{N}\ (r\le N/2),\quad (r-N)\frac{F_s}{N}\ (r>N/2)$$ $$\text{index } k = r + 1$$
