The Fourier Transform & Uncertainty

A Fourier series breaks a periodic signal into harmonics \(k\omega_0\). Let the period grow without bound and the harmonics merge into a continuum: every signal on the whole real line becomes an integral of sinusoids. This lesson defines the Fourier transform and its inverse, works out the standard pairs and properties, connects it to the Laplace transform and to filtering, derives the Nyquist sampling rate, and proves that no signal can be sharply concentrated in both time and frequency.

Section 11.1Definition and inverse

For \(g\) in \(L^1(-\infty,\infty)\) (or \(L^2\)), the Fourier transform and its inverse are

$$\hat G(\omega) = (\mathcal Fg)(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}g(t)\,dt,\qquad g(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\hat G(\omega)\,d\omega.$$

The inverse writes \(g\) as a continuous sum of sinusoids \(e^{i\omega t}\) with weights \(\hat G(\omega)\), so \(|\hat G(\omega)|\) is the spectrum of \(g\). For real \(g\), \(\hat G(-\omega) = \overline{\hat G(\omega)}\), so the magnitude is even and only \(\omega \ge 0\) needs plotting. Unlike the Laplace transform, \(g\) may be nonzero for \(t < 0\), but it must decay: \(e^t1_+(t)\) has a Laplace transform \(1/(s-1)\) and no Fourier transform.

Section 11.3Properties

Shifts and scaling

$$g(t - t_0)\to e^{-i\omega t_0}\hat G(\omega),\qquad e^{i\omega_0t}g(t)\to\hat G(\omega - \omega_0)$$ $$g(at)\to\frac{1}{|a|}\hat G\!\left(\frac\omega a\right),\qquad \cos(\omega_0t)g\to\tfrac12[\hat G(\omega-\omega_0) + \hat G(\omega+\omega_0)]$$

Calculus and products

$$\dot g\to i\omega\hat G(\omega),\qquad g\ast u = \int g(t-\sigma)u(\sigma)d\sigma\to\hat G\hat U$$ $$\int|g|^2dt = \frac1{2\pi}\int|\hat G|^2d\omega\ \ \text{(Parseval)}$$

Parseval's identity, with the weight \(\frac1{2\pi}\), says the transform preserves energy; it is the continuous version of \(\|f\|^2 = \sum|a_k|^2\). It also evaluates integrals: \(e^{-at}1_+\) has energy \(\frac1{2a}\), so \(\int\frac{d\omega}{a^2 + \omega^2} = \frac{\pi}{a}\).

Section 11.4Rational transforms and the Laplace transform

If \(g = 0\) for \(t < 0\) and its Laplace transform \(G\) is stable, then \(\hat G(\omega) = G(i\omega)\): the Fourier transform is the Laplace transform on the imaginary axis. To invert a rational \(\hat G(\omega)\), write it in terms of \(s = i\omega\) and expand in partial fractions. Poles in the left half-plane give terms \(e^{\lambda t}1_+(t)\) that live in \(t \ge 0\). Poles in the right half-plane give terms \(-e^{\lambda t}1_+(-t)\) that live in \(t \le 0\), since \(\mathcal F\{-e^{\lambda t}1_+(-t)\} = \frac{1}{i\omega - \lambda}\) when \(\Re\lambda > 0\). For example \(\frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2} = \frac{1}{a + i\omega} + \frac{1}{a - i\omega}\) gives \(e^{-at}\) for \(t>0\) plus \(e^{at}\) for \(t<0\), which is \(e^{-a|t|}\).

Section 11.5Transfer functions and ideal filters

For a stable system \(y = g\ast u\), so \(\hat Y(\omega) = G(i\omega)\hat U(\omega)\). Each frequency component of the input is multiplied by the frequency response, which is the steady-state rule of Lesson 9 applied to every \(\omega\) at once. The ideal low-pass filter (\(\hat G = 1\) on \(|\omega| < \omega_c\)) has impulse response

$$g(t) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c}e^{i\omega t}d\omega = \frac{\sin\omega_ct}{\pi t},$$

which is nonzero for \(t < 0\). The filter would respond before it is excited, so it is not causal and cannot be built. Butterworth filters (Lesson 10) are the practical approximation.

Section 11.6The Nyquist sampling rate

A signal is band-limited if \(\hat G(\omega) = 0\) for \(|\omega| > \omega_{\max}\). Sample it every \(T\) seconds. If

$$T < \frac{\pi}{\omega_{\max}}\qquad\big(\text{equivalently } F_s = 1/T > 2f_{\max}\big),$$

then the samples determine \(g\) exactly, through the Shannon interpolation formula

$$g(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}g(kT)\,\frac{\sin\big(\pi(t - kT)/T\big)}{\pi(t - kT)/T}.$$

Sampling makes the spectrum periodic with period \(2\pi/T\). If the copies overlap (\(T\) too large), high frequencies fold onto low ones. This is aliasing, the same effect seen with the DFT in Lesson 4. A tone at \(f\) Hz sampled at \(F_s\) appears at \(|f - \ell F_s|\) folded into \([0, F_s/2]\).

Section 11.7The uncertainty principle

Normalize \(\|g\| = 1\). Then \(|g(t)|^2\) and \(|\hat G(\omega)|^2/2\pi\) are probability densities (by Parseval). Their means \(\mu_g, \mu_G\) and standard deviations \(\sigma_g, \sigma_G\) measure where the signal is, and how spread out it is, in time and in frequency:

$$\sigma_g^2 = \int(t - \mu_g)^2|g(t)|^2dt,\qquad \sigma_G^2 = \frac1{2\pi}\int(\omega - \mu_G)^2|\hat G(\omega)|^2d\omega.$$

The Fourier uncertainty principle states

$$\sigma_g\,\sigma_G \ge \tfrac12,$$

with equality only for Gaussians \(g = ce^{-at^2 + bt}\). The proof is Cauchy–Schwarz applied to \((t - \mu_g)g\) and \(\dot g - i\mu_Gg\), using \(\sigma_G = \|\dot g - i\mu_Gg\|\) (Parseval and \(\mathcal F\dot g = i\omega\hat G\)). A short pulse must have a wide spectrum; a narrow-band signal must last a long time. For \(g = e^{-at^2}\), \(\sigma_g = \frac{1}{2\sqrt a}\) and \(\sigma_G = \sqrt a\), so the product is exactly \(\tfrac12\).

Section 11.8Worked examples

Worked examples

Find the Fourier transform of \(g = 1\) on \([0, 2]\), zero elsewhere.

Solution

It is the pulse on \([-1,1]\) delayed by 1: \(\hat G(\omega) = e^{-i\omega}\frac{2\sin\omega}{\omega}\). Directly: \(\int_0^2e^{-i\omega t}dt = \frac{1 - e^{-2i\omega}}{i\omega} = e^{-i\omega}\frac{e^{i\omega} - e^{-i\omega}}{i\omega}\). ✓ The magnitude is the same as the centered pulse; only the phase changes.

Find \(g(t)\) whose Fourier transform is \(\hat G(\omega) = \dfrac{6}{(2 + i\omega)(1 - i\omega)}\).

Solution

With \(s = i\omega\): \(\frac{6}{(s+2)(1-s)} = \frac{2}{s+2} + \frac{2}{1-s}\). The left pole \(-2\) gives \(2e^{-2t}\) for \(t \ge 0\). The term \(\frac{2}{1 - i\omega}\) is the transform of \(2e^{t}1_+(-t)\), which lives on \(t \le 0\). So \(g(t) = 2e^{-2t}\) for \(t > 0\) and \(2e^{t}\) for \(t < 0\).

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin^2\omega}{\omega^2}d\omega\).

Solution

The pulse on \([-1,1]\) has \(\hat G = 2\sin\omega/\omega\) and energy \(\int|g|^2 = 2\). Parseval: \(2 = \frac1{2\pi}\int\frac{4\sin^2\omega}{\omega^2}d\omega\), so the integral is \(\pi\).

Audio is band-limited to 20 kHz. What is the slowest sampling rate that avoids aliasing, and where does a 30 kHz tone appear if you sample at 44.1 kHz without filtering?

Solution

\(F_s > 2\cdot20 = 40\) kHz (CDs use 44.1 kHz). A 30 kHz tone is above \(F_s/2 = 22.05\) kHz and folds to \(44.1 - 30 = 14.1\) kHz. That is why an analog low-pass (anti-aliasing) filter comes before the sampler.

A Gaussian pulse \(e^{-at^2}\) has \(\sigma_g = 0.1\) s. Find \(a\) and \(\sigma_G\).

Solution

\(\sigma_g = \frac{1}{2\sqrt a} = 0.1\Rightarrow\sqrt a = 5\), \(a = 25\). Then \(\sigma_G = \sqrt a = 5\) rad/s, and \(\sigma_g\sigma_G = 0.5\), the minimum possible.
